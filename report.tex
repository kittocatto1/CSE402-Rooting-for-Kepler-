\section*{Summary of Findings}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Summary of Findings}

The project asked whether the higher formal convergence order of recent
with-memory Newton-type schemes survives contact with Kepler's specific
$\sin,\cos$ cost structure and with RadVel's real operating range of
$(e,M)$. It survives on order and on evaluation-count economy, and fails
on the two axes that decide practical use: wall-clock time and robustness
at high eccentricity.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \key{The methods are correctly implemented and behave as their
        papers claim.} Measured convergence orders reproduce the published
        values to within $1\%$ on every test function, and each scheme's eighth-order error
        \emph{constant} matches its paper's closed form to
        $\leq 3.5\times10^{-22}$ relative.
  \item \key{With-memory wins on iteration count and on the
        Kepler-specific cost model.} NWM11 reaches tolerance in a median
        of one iteration against Danby's two, at a median weighted cost of
        $6.44$ against Danby's $7.88$.
  \item \key{It loses on wall-clock time, by a factor of two to five.}
        The interpolation that makes the method cheap in transcendental
        calls is not free in arithmetic, and arithmetic is what the clock
        measures.
  \item \key{It loses badly on robustness in the pathological corner.}
        NWM9 and NWM11 fail on $4.7\%$ and $5.2\%$ of high-eccentricity
        points respectively, against Danby's $0.20\%$.
  \item \key{The proposal's central cost premise does not hold in this
        implementation.} A combined \texttt{sincos} evaluation was assumed
        to cost about one transcendental call; measured, it costs
        $2.44$, within timing noise of two separate calls ($2.06$). The discount the argument rests on is absent in CPython.
  \item \key{Downstream, none of it matters.} Solver-induced shifts in the
        fitted orbital parameters are three orders of magnitude smaller
        than the spread caused by measurement noise in the data.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{finding}{The short answer}
On this equation, Markley's closed-form solver is the best choice on every
axis measured: cheapest ($3.44$), fastest, and the only method with a
zero failure rate in the pathological corner. Among the iterative methods,
Danby remains the right production default. The with-memory schemes are a
genuine advance in iteration count and in transcendental-call economy, and
that advance does not convert into wall-clock speed or reliability here.
\end{finding}

\section{What Was Built}

The deliverable is a cost-instrumented benchmark harness
(\texttt{keplerbench}) in which every solver implements one interface,
every experiment runs through one pipeline, and every number in this
report is regenerated from a configuration file by a single command.

\subsection{Architecture}

All five solvers and all four starting guesses expose identical
interfaces, so the experiment runner loops over them blindly and no method
can gain an advantage from a different stopping rule. Solvers never call
\texttt{math.sin} directly; they evaluate the equation through a
\texttt{KeplerProblem} object that records what each call actually needed
--- how many distinct points were touched, how many required a full
$(\sin,\cos)$ pair against $\sin$ or $\cos$ alone, and how many
derivatives were synthesised from interpolation rather than evaluated.
That instrumentation is what makes Section~\ref{sec:cost} possible.

Reference roots come from an independent 50-digit bracketing solve in
\texttt{mpmath}, never from one of the methods under test.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

Each experiment writes its tables to \texttt{results/<experiment>/} with a
provenance sidecar recording the configuration fingerprint, the git
commit, and whether the working tree was clean when the numbers were
produced. Nothing in this report was transcribed by hand.

\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{notetitled}{Three corrections to the proposal's method table}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=0.35em]
  \item The with-memory schemes are \key{not derivative-free}. Both use
        $\varphi'(s_k)$ explicitly in their first sub-step. For Kepler this
        is favourable rather than otherwise: $f$ and $f'$ come from a
        single $(\sin,\cos)$ pair, which is exactly why the ``3 points,
        1 pair'' economy is achievable at all.
  \item NWM9 is \key{not the controlled sibling of NWM11}. They come from
        two different papers with two different eighth-order base methods
        and the parameter placed in different sub-steps. NWM11's true
        same-base control is \key{NWM10} (R-order 10), defined in the same
        paper as NWM11. This matters for interpreting
        Section~\ref{sec:robust}, where the two behave qualitatively
        differently.
  \item The phrase ``at no additional evaluation points per iteration''
        is misleading. Both schemes use three evaluation points per
        iteration against Newton's one. What costs nothing extra is the
        \emph{derivative information}, obtained by Hermite interpolation
        over points already paid for.
\end{enumerate}
\end{notetitled}

\section{Verification: the Gate Before the Benchmark}
\label{sec:verify}

The project's rule was that no solver enters the grid benchmark until its
measured convergence order matches its source paper's claim. This is what separates ``the method
underperforms'' from ``we mistyped the method''.

\subsection{Measured convergence order}

Each solver was run on the eight nonlinear test functions of the NWM11
paper, from the paper's own starting points, at 2000-digit working
precision. NWM9 comes from a different paper with its own examples, but
it was gated on the same NWM11 set; checking it on its own paper's
functions remains to be done (\texttt{experiments/verification.py}).

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Solver} & \textbf{Claimed} & \textbf{Measured (mean)} & \textbf{Range over 8 functions} & \textbf{Gate} \\
\midrule
Newton  & 2.0000  & 2.0000  & 2.0000 -- 2.0000   & PASS \\
Danby   & 4.0000  & 4.0000  & 4.0000 -- 4.0000   & PASS \\
NWM9    & 8.8989  & 8.9114  & 8.8746 -- 8.9816   & PASS \\
NWM11   & 10.7446 & 10.7605 & 10.7147 -- 10.8336 & PASS \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every measurement sits slightly \emph{above} the claim, which is correct:
both papers state their R-order as a lower bound (``at least'').

\begin{notetitled}{Why 2000 digits, and not 16}
An order-$p$ method multiplies its correct digits by $p$ each step. From
an error of $10^{-1}$, an order-9 method passes $10^{-9}$, $10^{-81}$,
$10^{-729}$. The estimator needs three consecutive usable errors to form
one estimate, so the working precision must outrun $p^3$ digits or the
sequence hits the floor before a single estimate exists. At 100 digits
NWM9 measured anywhere in $8.87$--$10.19$; at 2000 it settles to
$8.87$--$8.98$, and 3000 buys nothing further. Double precision cannot
measure these methods at all.
\end{notetitled}

\subsection{The order alone is not sufficient evidence}

Both papers' iteration formulas contain nested fractions whose PDF text
layer admits several readings, and for both schemes \emph{more than one
reading yields the correct order}. Measuring order 8 would therefore not
have detected a mis-transcription.

What does detect it is each paper's explicit error \emph{constant}. Each
scheme was driven for exactly one step from a point $10^{-24}$ from the
root and its eighth-order constant compared against the published closed
form:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Scheme} & \textbf{Published constant} & \textbf{Agreement (relative)} \\
\midrule
NWM9  & Eq.~(7): $c_2^2 K[(\alpha+c_2)K + c_4]$, $K = c_2+5c_2^2-c_3$ & $\leq 1.8\times10^{-22}$ \\
NWM11 & Eq.~(2.6): $(\alpha+c_2)^2 c_3 (c_2c_3 - c_4)$                & $\leq 3.5\times10^{-22}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A wrong grouping in NWM11's $w'(t_k)$ missed this constant by roughly 60
orders of magnitude while still converging at order 8. Three of the eight
test functions also had to be corrected: superscripts detach in the PDF
text layer, so $e^{s^2}$ extracts as $e^{s}$ followed by a stray $2$.
These were caught because the published roots did not zero the
mis-transcribed functions.

\subsection{Two-stage verification}

Freezing each scheme's self-accelerating parameters at zero must recover
the underlying memoryless method. Both do, measuring exactly $8.0000$ on
all eight functions, which attributes the lift to $8.8989$ and $10.7446$
to the memory rather than to a coincidence in the base method.

\subsection{Correctness: the right root, not just a root}

A solver that jumps to a different branch of Kepler's equation reports a
tiny residual and a converged flag while every downstream quantity is
wrong. Over 4{,}975 $(e,M)$ points spanning the full range:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Solver} & \textbf{Converged} & \textbf{Wrong root} & \textbf{Max $|E-E_{\mathrm{ref}}|$} \\
\midrule
Newton & 99.60\% & 0 & $3.38\times10^{-10}$ \\
Danby  & 99.72\% & 0 & $1.65\times10^{-10}$ \\
NWM9   & 98.15\% & 0 & $2.32\times10^{-10}$ \\
NWM11  & 99.50\% & 0 & $2.23\times10^{-10}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

No method converged to a wrong branch anywhere. Non-convergence is
reported honestly and is treated as a robustness result
(Section~\ref{sec:robust}), not as a correctness failure.

\section{Kepler-Specific Cost Accounting}
\label{sec:cost}

\subsection{Measured per-iteration cost}

Counted from the instrumented code rather than transcribed from the
papers:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{Eval.\ points} & \textbf{$(\sin,\cos)$ pairs} & \textbf{$\sin$ only} & \textbf{Synth.\ derivatives} & \textbf{Order} \\
\midrule
Newton  & 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 2.0000 \\
Danby   & 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 4.0000 \\
Markley & 2 & 1 & 1 & 0 & --- \\
NWM9    & 3 & 1 & 3 & 3 & 8.8989 \\
NWM11   & 3 & 1 & 3 & 3 & 10.7446 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\key{The proposal's claim for NWM11 --- three evaluation points, only one
full $(\sin,\cos)$ pair per iteration --- is confirmed by measurement.}
Both with-memory schemes obtain three derivatives per iteration by
interpolation rather than by evaluation.

\begin{note}
\textbf{One count in this table is an artefact of the harness, not of the
methods.} The shared iteration loop calls $f(E)$ itself after each step to
compute the residual for the stopping test. That inflates the
$\sin$-only column by exactly one per iteration for \emph{every} solver
--- which is why Newton and Danby show a $\sin$-only cost of 1 despite
needing nothing beyond their single $(\sin,\cos)$ pair. The with-memory
schemes' own $\sin$-only cost is therefore 2, not 3. The inflation is
uniform, so it does not distort comparisons, but it must be subtracted
before any of these figures is quoted against a paper's table.
\end{note}

\subsection{The cost premise, measured}

The proposal's argument rests on a specific empirical claim: that one
\texttt{sincos} call returns both $\sin E$ and $\cos E$ ``at roughly the
cost of one transcendental evaluation''. Measured on the project machine,
with $\sin$-only normalised to $1.0$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Operation} & \textbf{Relative cost} & \textbf{Proposal assumed} \\
\midrule
$\sin$ only                      & 1.000 & 1 \\
$\cos$ only                      & 1.06 & 1 \\
$(\sin,\cos)$ pair               & \textbf{2.44} & $\approx 1$ \\
Synthesised derivative (Hermite) & 1.35 & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

These are the weights every weighted cost in this report is computed with
(\texttt{DEFAULT\_WEIGHTS} in \texttt{evaluation/cost\_model.py}). For
example, Newton's $3.44$ per iteration is one pair plus one $\sin$. They
are machine-dependent and noisy: a repeat measurement with
\texttt{measure\_weights\_with\_spread} spread the pair over roughly
$1.1$--$2.7$ across seven runs.

\begin{finding}{The sincos discount does not exist in this implementation}
A combined $(\sin,\cos)$ evaluation costs $2.44$ against $2.06$ for two
separate calls. Given the run-to-run spread, that difference is within
noise: the pair costs about the same as two calls, not one. In pure Python
there is no fused \texttt{sincos} instruction to exploit: each call
crosses the interpreter boundary separately.

This does not invalidate the proposal's reasoning, but it relocates it. In
a compiled implementation such as RadVel's Cython kernel, where a genuine
fused \texttt{sincos} is available, the discount is real and the
with-memory economy would translate. In our harness it does not, and this
is the single clearest explanation for why the methods that win on
weighted cost lose on the clock in Section~\ref{sec:grid}.

\medskip
Both costings belong in any conclusion drawn from this work. Reporting
only the weighted cost would overstate the with-memory case; reporting
only the wall clock would understate it.
\end{finding}

\pipelinefigure{figures/cost/cost_breakdown.pdf}{1.0}{%
Per-iteration cost under both costings. \emph{Left}: the weights measured in
this CPython harness, where Newton, Danby and Markley each cost $3.44$ and
both with-memory schemes $9.49$. \emph{Right}: the call count a genuine fused
\texttt{sincos} would charge in C --- $2.00$ against $4.00$. The gap between
the two panels is the whole of Section~\ref{sec:cost}'s argument: which
costing you adopt changes the ratio between the classical and with-memory
groups from $2.8\times$ to $2.0\times$. Both include the harness's own
residual check, one $\sin$ per iteration.}

\section{Grid Benchmark}
\label{sec:grid}

Every solver--guess combination was run over a 7{,}400-point $(e,M)$ grid
combining a uniform block, a logarithmically-spaced pathological corner
and a RadVel-realistic sample: 17 combinations $\times$ 7{,}400 points
$=$ \key{125{,}800 instrumented solves}. Markley ignores the starting
guess and is run once under the label \texttt{n/a}.

\subsection{Headline comparison}

Each iterative solver is shown with its best-performing guess.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Solver} & \textbf{Guess} & \textbf{Mean iter.} & \textbf{Median cost} & \textbf{Median wall (µs)} & \textbf{Converged} \\
\midrule
Markley & n/a       & 0.00 & \textbf{3.44} & \textbf{3} & 100.00\% \\
NWM11   & canonical & \textbf{1.38} & 6.44 & 5 & 99.89\% \\
NWM9    & napier    & 1.44 & 6.44 & 7 & 100.00\% \\
Danby   & napier    & 1.94 & 7.88 & 3 & 100.00\% \\
Newton  & napier    & 3.21 & 11.32 & 3 & 100.00\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Three separate stories are visible in those columns.

\key{Iteration count.} NWM11 needs a median of one iteration where Danby
needs two and Newton needs three or four. The higher order is real and it
shows up exactly where theory says it should.

\key{Weighted cost.} On the Kepler-specific currency the proposal argued
for, the with-memory schemes beat Danby: $6.44$ against $7.88$, about
$18\%$ cheaper. \emph{This is the proposal's hypothesis being confirmed.}

\key{Wall-clock time.} The ranking inverts. Markley, Danby and Newton all
land at 3\,µs per solve; NWM9 and NWM11 take 5--8\,µs, and NWM11 with the
weakest guess takes 17\,µs. The interpolation machinery costs no
transcendental calls but a great deal of arithmetic, and the weighted cost
model --- by construction --- does not count arithmetic.

\subsection{The guess layer}

The starting guess is a larger effect than the choice of iteration for the
weaker solvers. Newton's mean iteration count moves from $3.83$ with
$E_0 = M$ to $3.21$ with Napier's formula, and its convergence rate from
$99.84\%$ to $100\%$. For NWM9 the effect on reliability is starker:
$99.39\%$ with the simple start against $100\%$ with Napier's.

This vindicates the proposal's decision to treat the guess as an
independent experimental factor: reporting NWM9 with a poor start would
have understated it substantially.

proposal predicted the generic index would exhibit on this equation.}

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robust}

Failure rates split by region, where the pathological corner is defined
once for the whole project as $e > 0.9$ and $M < 0.1$.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Solver} & \textbf{Corner points} & \textbf{Corner failure} & \textbf{Ordinary points} & \textbf{Ordinary failure} \\
\midrule
Markley & 381  & \textbf{0.000\%} & 7{,}019  & \textbf{0.000\%} \\
Danby   & 1{,}524 & 0.197\% & 28{,}076 & 0.004\% \\
Newton  & 1{,}524 & 0.919\% & 28{,}076 & 0.025\% \\
NWM9    & 1{,}524 & 4.724\% & 28{,}076 & 0.057\% \\
NWM11   & 1{,}524 & \textbf{5.249\%} & 28{,}076 & \textbf{0.000\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{finding}{Where the with-memory methods actually lose}
In the ordinary operating region NWM11 is flawless --- zero failures in
28{,}076 solves, better than Danby. In the pathological corner it fails
$5.2\%$ of the time, roughly \textbf{25 times} Danby's rate.

The mechanism is structural rather than incidental. Both schemes place a
self-accelerating parameter additively on a denominator, and as
$e \rightarrow 1$ with $M \rightarrow 0$ the derivative
$f'(E) = 1 - e\cos E$ collapses towards zero. The parameter, tuned on the
assumption that the iterate is already near the root, then perturbs a
denominator that is itself nearly zero.

This is confirmed by construction: with the parameter frozen at zero, the
memoryless base methods converge at points where the with-memory versions
diverge. The failure is caused by the memory, not by the base scheme.
\end{finding}

pathological corner occupies the top left rather than a sliver. Failures are
almost entirely confined to that corner. The totals track the regional rates
above: Markley $0$, Danby $4$, Newton $21$, NWM11 $68$, NWM9 $78$. Points at
exactly $e=0$ or $M=0$ cannot be drawn on log axes and are noted in each

\section{Downstream: Does Any of It Matter?}
\label{sec:downstream}

Solver error was traced through $M \rightarrow E \rightarrow \nu
\rightarrow v_r$ into refitted orbital parameters, with each of our
solvers patched into RadVel in place of its own compiled solver. A call
counter asserts after every fit that the patch actually fired.

\key{Design: injection-recovery on real cadence.} The observation times,
the per-point uncertainties and the three-instrument structure are the
real HD~164922 data (401 HIRES points). The velocities are
\emph{not}: they are replaced by a known, moderately eccentric orbit
($P = 20.885258$\,d, $e = 0.35$, $\omega = 1.0$\,rad, $K = 5$\,m\,s$^{-1}$)
plus Gaussian noise at the real uncertainties. Fitting HD~164922's own
velocities with a single-planet model would give a near-circular,
optimiser-dependent eccentricity (it has four planets), and near $e=0$
the Kepler solve barely matters, so the study would measure nothing. The
injection also gives a known answer to recover. The fit varies $P$, the
time of conjunction, $\sqrt{e}\cos\omega$, $\sqrt{e}\sin\omega$ and
$\log K$ (RadVel's recommended basis), plus one jitter term per
instrument. Before the final fit, a 30-point multi-start over $(e,\omega)$
using RadVel's native solver picks the starting point. The fit recovers
$e = 0.342$, $\omega = 0.997$ and $K = 5.03$, all within $0.5\sigma$ of the
injected values, identically for all five solvers. Each fit performs about
$1.3\times10^{5}$ Kepler solves.

Only the fit made with the solver under test is timed, not the multi-start

\subsection{The error budget}

The question is not how large the solver-induced parameter shift is, but
how it compares to the two uncertainties that are already present.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Solver shift} & \textbf{Noise spread} & \textbf{MCMC posterior} & \textbf{Noise / solver} \\
 & \footnotesize(worst case) & \footnotesize(Monte Carlo) & \footnotesize($1\sigma$) & \\
\midrule
$P$ (d)  & $1.2\times10^{-8}$ & $5.04\times10^{-4}$ & $6.36\times10^{-4}$ & $4.3\times10^{4}$ \\
$e$      & $1.1\times10^{-5}$ & $1.65\times10^{-2}$ & $1.75\times10^{-2}$ & $1.5\times10^{3}$ \\
$\omega$ (rad) & $2.2\times10^{-5}$ & $5.03\times10^{-2}$ & $5.52\times10^{-2}$ & $2.2\times10^{3}$ \\
$K$ (m\,s$^{-1}$) & $3.5\times10^{-5}$ & $9.93\times10^{-2}$ & $1.03\times10^{-1}$ & $2.8\times10^{3}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The solver shift is the largest absolute shift over all five solvers and
all tolerances, measured from each solver's own $10^{-14}$ fit. In every
parameter that worst case is Newton at $10^{-4}$. The noise spread is the
standard deviation over 50 refits of fresh noise realisations. The MCMC
width comes from one RadVel run with the native solver (2000 steps).

\begin{finding}{Solver choice is not a source of error in practice}
Even at the loosest tolerance tested ($10^{-4}$, far looser than any
sensible production setting), the shift a solver induces in the fitted
parameters is \textbf{three orders of magnitude smaller} than the scatter
caused by measurement noise in the data. The noise-driven spread
independently reproduces RadVel's own MCMC posterior width to within
$10\%$ for $e$, $\omega$ and $K$ ($26\%$ for $P$), which is a useful
consistency check on the Monte Carlo study itself.

Newton at $10^{-4}$ is the only setting with a genuine solver effect
($6\times10^{-4}\sigma$ in $e$). As a second-order method it stops with an
error in $E$ of about $10^{-4}$. Danby, NWM9 and NWM11 are already at full
double precision by the time they pass even a $10^{-4}$ residual check,
and Markley ignores the tolerance entirely, so its six fits are
bit-identical. At every other setting the shift is at most $2\times10^{-5}\sigma$ and does
not shrink as the tolerance tightens. That residue is the resolution floor
of RadVel's Powell optimiser (\texttt{xtol}$\,=10^{-8}$), not solver error,
so the study bounds the solver effect there rather than measuring it. The
shift at $10^{-14}$ is zero by construction, since that is the reference.

\medskip
The practical consequence: \emph{choose the Kepler solver for speed and
robustness, not for accuracy.} Accuracy stopped being the binding
constraint long before it reached the astronomy.
\end{finding}

\subsection{Real-data checks}

Because the main study uses injected velocities, all five solvers were
also fitted to three datasets' \emph{real}, unmodified velocities. These
checks test agreement between solvers, not recovery of a known orbit.
For the two transiting planets the period and transit time come from
photometry and are held fixed, as in RadVel's own example setups:
\begin{itemize}
  \item K2-24~b: $P = 20.885258$\,d, $t_c = 2072.79438$.
  \item K2-131~b: $P = 0.3693038$\,d, $t_c = 2457582.9360$ [9].
\end{itemize}
HD~164922~b does not transit, so only its period ($1207$\,d) is fixed. Left
free, sparse real data let the fit wander: $P$ jumped to an alias for
K2-131 ($3.02$\,d), and $e$ ran off to $\approx 0.95$ for K2-24.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Dataset} & \textbf{Points} & \textbf{Fitted $e$} & \textbf{Spread across 5 solvers} & \textbf{Better than circular?} \\
\midrule
K2-131 (1 planet)    & 70  & 0.135 & $2.8\times10^{-6}$ & no ($\Delta\ln\mathcal{L} \approx 0.00$) \\
HD~164922 (4 planets) & 401 & 0.122 & $1.5\times10^{-8}$ & marginally ($0.08$) \\
K2-24 (2 planets)    & 32  & 0.908 & $7.1\times10^{-9}$ & no ($-0.11$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

All five solvers agree to within $3\times10^{-6}$ in $e$ on every real
dataset. The fitted eccentricities themselves are not orbit measurements:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{K2-131:} $e \approx 0.13$ fits the data exactly as well as
        the published, tidally circularised $e \approx 0$. The RV data
        alone cannot separate them.
  \item \textbf{HD~164922 and K2-24:} both are multi-planet systems fitted
        with a single-planet model. For K2-24 the optimiser lands at a
        high-$e$ solution slightly \emph{worse} than a circular orbit.
\end{itemize}

\section{Synthesis}

Returning to the question the proposal posed --- under which conditions of
eccentricity, mean anomaly, and Kepler-specific evaluation cost is each
class of method preferable?

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.4cm} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Axis} & \textbf{Verdict} \\
\midrule
Convergence order & With-memory wins decisively and as advertised. NWM11 measures 10.76 against Danby's 4.00, and holds it across the whole eccentricity range. \\
Iteration count & With-memory wins: a median of 1 iteration against Danby's 2 and Newton's 3--4. \\
Kepler-specific weighted cost & With-memory wins, $6.44$ against $7.88$ --- about $18\%$ cheaper than RadVel's production solver. \\
Wall-clock time & With-memory \emph{loses}, by 2--5$\times$. The interpolation is free in transcendental calls and expensive in arithmetic. \\
Robustness, ordinary region & Tie, or a slight with-memory win: NWM11 records zero failures in 28{,}076 solves. \\
Robustness, pathological corner & With-memory \emph{loses} badly: $5.2\%$ failure against Danby's $0.20\%$. \\
Downstream accuracy & Irrelevant. All methods are $10^{3}$ times more accurate than the data justify. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

The trade-off falls exactly where the proposal anticipated it might --- the
newer variants win on order and iteration count and lose on per-iteration
cost and high-eccentricity robustness --- but with one result that was not
anticipated. \key{The Kepler-specific cost advantage, which was the
project's whole reason for expecting the with-memory schemes to compete,
is real in the cost model and absent from the clock.} The model assumes a
\texttt{sincos} discount that this implementation does not provide.

That is not a negative result about the methods. It is a statement about
where they would pay off: in a compiled kernel with a genuine fused
\texttt{sincos}, the $18\%$ weighted-cost advantage should convert into
real time. Testing that claim requires a compiled implementation and is
the natural continuation of this work.

Markley's closed-form solver, meanwhile, was the quiet winner throughout:
cheapest on the cost model at $3.44$, joint-fastest on the clock, and the
only method in the study with a zero failure rate anywhere on the grid.

\section{Threats to Validity}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \key{Pure-Python arithmetic.} Every wall-clock figure is measured
        through the CPython interpreter, where arithmetic is expensive
        relative to transcendental calls and no fused \texttt{sincos}
        exists. This is the single largest threat, and it works
        specifically against the with-memory schemes.
  \item \key{The residual-evaluation artefact.} The shared loop's own
        $f(E)$ call inflates every solver's $\sin$-only count by one per
        iteration. It is uniform across methods and does not affect
        rankings, but the absolute per-iteration figures in
        Section~\ref{sec:cost} are one $\sin$ high.
  \item \key{One injected orbit.} The error-propagation and Monte Carlo
        studies use a single injected, moderately eccentric orbit
        ($e = 0.35$) on HD~164922's real cadence and noise, not HD~164922's
        own velocities. The three-orders-of-magnitude margin is wide enough
        that the conclusion is unlikely to depend on this choice. However,
        higher-eccentricity injections, where $\mathrm{d}\nu/\mathrm{d}E$
        is largest, were not run. The real-data checks on three systems
        test only whether the solvers agree with each other.
  \item \key{Optimiser resolution floor.} Below tolerance $10^{-4}$ the
        measured shifts ($\lesssim 2\times10^{-5}\sigma$) are set by the
        stopping precision of RadVel's Powell fit. They are an upper bound
        on the solver's effect, not a measurement of it.
  \item \key{Small MCMC budget.} The posterior widths come from a single
        2000-step MCMC run, which is not seeded, so they vary by a few per
        cent between runs. They agree with the Monte Carlo spread,
        but are not publication-grade on their own.
  \item \key{Estimator artefacts at extreme eccentricity.} Several entries
        in the order-versus-eccentricity table are measurement artefacts
        rather than method behaviour, as noted in
        Section~\ref{sec:robust}. They are reported rather than removed,
        with the unreliable points marked.
  \item \key{NWM9's degradation is single-sourced.} The collapse beyond
        $e \approx 0.99$ has been reproduced across precisions but not
        cross-checked against an independent implementation of the same
        published scheme.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

Five root finders were implemented against one instrumented interface,
each verified against its source paper's convergence order and error
constant before being benchmarked, then run over 125{,}800 solves spanning
the full $(e,M)$ range and traced downstream into refitted orbital
parameters.

The 2024--2025 with-memory schemes do what their papers claim. On Kepler's
equation they reach tolerance in fewer iterations than any iterative
alternative and, on the Kepler-specific cost model the proposal argued
for, they are about $18\%$ cheaper than RadVel's production solver. That
advantage does not survive translation into wall-clock time in a
pure-Python implementation, because the cost model's central assumption
--- a cheap fused \texttt{sincos} --- does not hold there. They are also
markedly less reliable in the high-eccentricity corner, by a structural
mechanism traced to the accelerating parameter itself.

For RadVel's actual operating range the practical recommendation is
unchanged: Danby, or Markley where a closed form is acceptable. The
with-memory schemes are worth revisiting in a compiled kernel, where the
cost structure the proposal described genuinely exists.

The outcome was not fixed in advance, and it did not come out uniformly in
either direction. That was the point.
