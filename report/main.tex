\begin{abstract}
Kepler's equation, $E - e\sin E = M$, is solved every time an orbit is
evaluated, about 130{,}000 times in one radial-velocity fit. Two recent
papers propose with-memory iterative methods, NWM9 (2024) and NWM11
(2025), that reach convergence orders $8.90$ and $10.74$ with no extra
function evaluations, but neither tests them on Kepler's equation. We
reimplemented both, confirmed their orders at 2000 digits, and compared
them with Newton's, Danby's and Markley's methods in 125{,}800 solves over
7{,}400 values of eccentricity $e$ and mean anomaly $M$ and four starting
guesses. The new methods need the fewest iterations (1.38 for NWM11
against 1.94 for Danby), but in the corner $e > 0.9$, $M < 0.1$ they fail
33 to 37 times as often as Danby. We traced these failures to the
self-accelerating parameter. It divides by $f'(E) = 1 - e\cos E$, so near
$f' = 0$ its correction grows as large as the derivative it adjusts. We
propose a one-line safeguard that keeps the parameter only when its
correction is at most half of $|f'|$. Safeguarded NWM11 cuts its failures
from 74 to 3 (Danby has 2) and keeps both its iteration count and its
measured order of $10.74$ up to $e = 0.999$. The starting guess matters as
much as the method: Napier's guess lowers NWM9's mean iteration count from
2.37 to 1.44. Even in the worst case, solver error moves the fitted orbit
about 1{,}500 times less than measurement noise does.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Base Paper Review}

\subsection{The problem}

A planet's position along its orbit is described by the mean anomaly $M$,
which grows linearly with time, and the eccentric anomaly $E$, which fixes
where the planet actually is. The two are linked by Kepler's equation
\begin{equation}
f(E) = E - e\sin E - M = 0, \qquad 0 \le e < 1,
\label{eq:kepler}
\end{equation}
where $e$ is the orbital eccentricity. The equation has no closed-form
solution, so it is solved numerically every time an orbit is evaluated.
RadVel \cite{radvel}, a standard toolkit for fitting exoplanet orbits to
radial-velocity data, solves it once per observation for every
likelihood evaluation. In our experiments a single maximum-likelihood fit in RadVel needed
about $1.3\times10^{5}$ solves (Section~\ref{sec:downstream}), and a
Markov chain Monte Carlo run evaluates the likelihood many more times
than one fit does. The speed and reliability of
the solver therefore matter in practice.

Two properties of Equation~\eqref{eq:kepler} matter for the rest of this
report.
First, its derivative $f'(E) = 1 - e\cos E$ approaches zero as $e \to 1$
and $M \to 0$, because the root $E$ is then close to zero and
$\cos E \approx 1$. Every Newton-type step divides by $f'$, so this
corner of the $(e, M)$ plane is where solvers struggle. Second, $f$ and
$f'$ are built from $\sin E$ and $\cos E$ at the same point, so a method
that needs both pays for one pair of trigonometric calls, not two separate
evaluations. A method's cost on this equation is therefore not the same as
its cost on a generic nonlinear equation.

RadVel's own solver is Danby's fourth-order method \cite{danby}, written in
C following Murray and Dermott \cite{murray}, with a residual tolerance of
$10^{-12}$ \cite{radvelsrc}. That is the method a new solver has to beat.

\subsection{The base papers}
\label{sec:base}

Our base papers are two articles by Mittal, Panday and co-authors
\cite{nwm9,nwm11}. Both start from an optimal eighth-order, three-step
method and raise its order with \emph{self-accelerating parameters}:
quantities that are recomputed every iteration from points the method has
already evaluated. Because the method reuses information from earlier
iterations, it is called a \emph{with-memory} method.

\textbf{NWM9} \cite{nwm9} adds one parameter, $\alpha_n$, to the third
step of the eighth-order method of Matthies et al.\ \cite{matthies}. The
paper proves an R-order of at least $8.8989$ and an efficiency index that rises from
$1.6818$ to $1.7272$.

\textbf{NWM11} \cite{nwm11} adds two parameters, $\alpha_k$ and $\beta_k$,
to the first and third steps of the eighth-order method of Solaiman and
Hashim \cite{solaiman}. The paper proves an R-order of at least $10.7446$
and an efficiency index that rises from $1.6818$ to $1.8105$.

In both methods the parameters are computed from derivatives of Hermite
interpolating polynomials fitted through the current and previous iterates. Those points
have already been evaluated, so the higher order costs no new function
evaluations. That is the central claim of both papers.

\subsection{What the base papers leave open}

Both papers test their methods on eight smooth scalar functions, each from
a single starting point, and measure cost with the efficiency index
$\rho^{1/\gamma}$, where $\rho$ is the order and $\gamma$ counts the
function and derivative evaluations per iteration. This leaves four questions open for Kepler's equation.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cost.} The efficiency index charges $f$ and $f'$ as two
      evaluations and an interpolated derivative as none. On Kepler's
      equation $f$ and $f'$ come from one $(\sin, \cos)$ pair, and in our
      Python code an interpolated derivative costs more than a $\sin$ call.
\item \textbf{Behaviour across the domain.} Eight test functions say
      nothing about how a method behaves across the whole $(e, M)$ plane,
      and in particular near $e \to 1$, $M \to 0$ where $f' \to 0$.
\item \textbf{Robustness.} Neither paper reports how often the methods
      fail to converge, or why.
\item \textbf{Relevance.} Neither paper asks whether a higher order changes
      anything in an application whose data are noisy.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Our contributions}

Our objective is to test whether the higher order of NWM9 and NWM11 pays
off on Kepler's equation, and to repair the methods where they fail. We
keep the published formulas exactly as given and change them only after
finding where they break. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A verified reimplementation.} Both methods reproduce their
      published orders at 2000-digit precision. We also show that matching
      the order is not enough to prove a correct transcription, and that
      each paper's error constant is the check that works
      (Section~\ref{sec:verify}).
\item \textbf{A Kepler-specific benchmark} of five solvers and four
      starting guesses over 125{,}800 solves, with cost counted in measured
      units of trigonometric work (Section~\ref{sec:grid}). It shows that
      the starting guess can matter more than the method: replacing
      $E_0 = M$ with Napier's guess cuts NWM9's mean iteration count from
      2.37 to 1.44 and removes all of its failures (Section~\ref{sec:guess}).
\item \textbf{An explanation of the failures.} The with-memory methods fail
      in the corner because of the self-accelerating parameter, not the
      underlying eighth-order method. We show this directly by switching
      the memory off (Section~\ref{sec:failure}).
\item \textbf{An improvement to the base method.} A safeguard on the
      parameter that cuts NWM11's failures from 74 to 3, against Danby's 2,
      without changing its order or its iteration count
      (Section~\ref{sec:fix}).
\item \textbf{A downstream check} showing how far solver error travels
      into a fitted orbit, compared with the noise in real data
      (Section~\ref{sec:downstream}).
\end{enumerate}

All code, configuration files, result tables and figure scripts are public
at \url{https://github.com/kittocatto1/CSE402-Rooting-for-Kepler-}.

\subsection{The with-memory iteration}
\label{sec:nwm}

We use NWM11 to show the structure; NWM9 is built the same way with one
parameter instead of two. Starting from $s_k$, one NWM11 iteration is
\begin{align}
v_k     &= s_k - \frac{f(s_k)}{f'(s_k) + \alpha_k f(s_k)}, \label{eq:step1}\\
t_k     &= v_k - \frac{f(v_k)}{q}
           - \frac{2 f(v_k)^2 q R}{4q^4 - 4f(v_k)q^2R + f(v_k)^2R^2}, \notag\\
s_{k+1} &= t_k - \frac{f(t_k)}{w' + \beta_k f(t_k)}, \label{eq:step3}
\end{align}
where $q$, $R$ and $w'$ are divided-difference estimates of $f'(v_k)$,
$f''(v_k)$ and $f'(t_k)$ built from values already in hand
\cite{nwm11}. The parameters are
$\alpha_k = -H_5''(s_k)/(2f'(s_k))$ and
$\beta_k = H_7''''(t_k)/(4H_6'''(v_k)) + \alpha_k$, where $H_5$, $H_6$ and
$H_7$ are Hermite polynomials through the current and previous iterates.
With $\alpha_k = \beta_k = 0$ the scheme is the eighth-order method of
Solaiman and Hashim. Each iteration evaluates $f$ at three points but
needs $f'$ only at $s_k$, so it uses one $(\sin, \cos)$ pair and two lone
$\sin$ calls.

Two implementation details decide whether the parameters come out right.
The node lists repeat a point, for example
\[
[\,s_k,\ s_k,\ t_{k-1},\ v_{k-1},\ s_{k-1},\ s_{k-1}\,],
\]
which makes an ordinary divided difference $0/0$. Our interpolation code therefore uses the
confluent (Hermite) form and takes $f'$ at a repeated node. The papers
also need the derivative $H^{(k)}$ itself, not the Taylor coefficient
$H^{(k)}/k!$. Using the coefficient makes the parameter wrong by a factor
$k!$, yet the method still converges, so the mistake would not show up as
a crash.

\subsection{Measuring convergence order}
\label{sec:coc}

We check each claimed order instead of assuming it, using the
computational order of convergence
\begin{equation}
p \approx \frac{\ln|\varepsilon_{n+1}/\varepsilon_{n}|}
               {\ln|\varepsilon_{n}/\varepsilon_{n-1}|},
\qquad \varepsilon_n = |x_n - x^{*}|,
\end{equation}
which needs three consecutive errors that are still above the precision
floor. A method of order 9 starting at an error of $10^{-1}$ reaches
$10^{-9}$, then $10^{-81}$, then $10^{-729}$. In double precision only two
of those errors are visible, which is not enough for one estimate. We
therefore measure order at 2000 decimal digits with \texttt{mpmath}
\cite{mpmath}; the same solver code runs on both floats and
\texttt{mpmath} numbers. Everywhere else the solvers run in ordinary
double precision.

\subsection{Counting cost on Kepler's equation}
\label{sec:costmodel}

Counting iterations is unfair, because one NWM11 iteration does far more
work than one Newton iteration. The efficiency index
is also a poor fit here. It counts $f$ and $f'$ as two evaluations,
although on Kepler's equation they come from one $(\sin,\cos)$ pair, and
it counts an interpolated derivative as free. Every solver in our code
evaluates $f$ through a shared problem object that records what each call
needed. We then price each kind of call in units of one \texttt{sin} call,
timed on the benchmark machine (Table~\ref{tab:weights}).

\subsection{A safeguard for the self-accelerating parameter}
\label{sec:guard}

Each parameter enters a denominator as a correction added to a
derivative: $f'(s_k) + \alpha_k f(s_k)$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:step1}, and
$w' + \beta_k f(t_k)$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:step3}. The papers derive the
parameters assuming the iterate is already close to the root. There $f$ is
tiny and the correction is a small adjustment. Far from the root, or where
$f'$ is itself close to zero, the correction can be as large as the
derivative it is meant to adjust, and the step is thrown far off.
This happens because the parameter has $f'$ in its own denominator. In NWM11,
$\alpha_k = -H_5''(s_k)/(2f'(s_k))$, so the correction measured against
the derivative is
\[
\frac{|\alpha_k f(s_k)|}{|f'(s_k)|}
  = \frac{|H_5''(s_k)\, f(s_k)|}{2 f'(s_k)^2}.
\]
This ratio grows like $1/f'^2$ as $f' \to 0$, while the Newton step
$f/f'$ grows only like $1/f'$. NWM9's $\alpha_n$ contains the same term
$-H_5''/(2f')$ \cite{nwm9}.

Our safeguard tests this directly. In each iteration, a parameter $\lambda$
(standing for $\alpha_k$, $\beta_k$ or NWM9's $\alpha_n$) is used only if
\begin{equation}
|\lambda\, f| \le c\, |f'(s_k)|, \qquad c = \tfrac{1}{2},
\label{eq:guard}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the function value the parameter multiplies and $f'(s_k)$ is
the one derivative the iteration actually evaluates. We use $f'(s_k)$ for
$\beta_k$ too, because $w'$ estimates the same derivative at a nearby
point. Once the memory is in use, the test for NWM11's $\alpha_k$ is
exactly $|H_5''(s_k)\, f(s_k)| \le f'(s_k)^2$. It passes when $|f|$ is
small compared with $f'^2/|H_5''|$, which is the near-root situation the
papers' derivation assumes. If the test fails,
that parameter is set to zero for that iteration, so that sub-step is the
same as in the memoryless eighth-order method. The test costs two multiplications and
no new evaluations.

The safeguard cannot lower the order. Near a simple root, $f \to 0$ while
$f'(s_k) \to f'(E^*) \ne 0$, and the parameters stay bounded, so
after some iteration $|\lambda f|$ stays below $c|f'|$ and the test passes
every time. From then on the guarded method takes exactly the published
steps, so it has the published asymptotic order. We confirm this numerically in
Section~\ref{sec:fix}. For $\alpha_k$, $c = 1/2$ keeps the denominator
$f'(s_k) + \alpha_k f(s_k)$ between $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac32$ times
$f'(s_k)$, with the same sign, so the parameter can at most double the
Newton step and can never reverse it. Every number in this report uses
$c = 1/2$.

\subsection{Starting guesses}
\label{sec:guesses}

Every iterative method needs a first estimate $E_0$. Both base papers
prove their orders only for a start close enough to the root
\cite{nwm9,nwm11}, and each of their test functions starts from one fixed
point close to its root. On Kepler's equation the guess decides how near
the start is, so we treat it as a separate factor and test four:
$E_0 = M$ (\emph{simple});
$E_0 = M + e\sin M$ (\emph{canonical}, the first term of the series
solution);
$E_0 = M + 0.85\,e\,\mathrm{sign}(\sin M)$ (\emph{radvel}, the guess in
RadVel's code \cite{radvelsrc}); and
$E_0 = e\sin M + \max\{M,\ e(\sin M + 0.591)\}$ (\emph{napier}, found by
symbolic regression \cite{napier}).
Markley's method \cite{markley} is a closed-form starter followed by one
fixed correction, so it takes no guess and runs once per point.

\section{Implementation and Experiments}

\subsection{Code structure}
\label{sec:code}

The code is a Python package, \texttt{keplerbench}, built on NumPy
\cite{numpy}, SciPy \cite{scipy}, pandas \cite{pandas} and Matplotlib
\cite{matplotlib}; the radial-velocity study uses RadVel \cite{radvel},
whose MCMC runs on emcee \cite{emcee}. Every solver and every
starting guess implements one shared interface, so the experiment runner
treats them all the same way and every iterative solver uses the same
stopping rule: stop when $|f(E)| \le 10^{-14}$, or give up after 50
iterations. Solvers never call \texttt{math.sin} directly. They evaluate
Equation~\eqref{eq:kepler} through a \texttt{KeplerProblem} object that
counts every $(\sin,\cos)$ pair and every lone $\sin$ or $\cos$. Because
this counting happens outside the solver, a solver cannot under-report its
trigonometric work. The with-memory solvers also log each interpolated
derivative on the same counter. The cost of a solve leaves out the
starting guess, which costs one $\sin M$ call for every guess except
$E_0 = M$. Table~\ref{tab:solvers} lists the
solvers.

\subsection{Reference roots}

Accuracy is measured against roots computed separately in \texttt{mpmath}
at 50 decimal digits with a bracketing method (Anderson--Bj\"orck, with
bisection as a fallback). A root is accepted only if its residual is below
$10^{-40}$. This code shares nothing
with the solvers under test, so no solver is graded against itself.

\subsection{The test grid}
\label{sec:gridsetup}

We test on 7{,}400 $(e, M)$ points drawn in three different ways
(Table~\ref{tab:grid-sets}). The even grid covers the whole domain fairly.
The corner set is log-spaced, because equal steps would put almost no
points near $e \to 1$, $M \to 0$. The RadVel set follows the eccentricity
distribution of real exoplanets \cite{kipping}. The even grid stops at
$M = \pi$ because the solution is symmetric about it:
$E(2\pi - M) = 2\pi - E(M)$.

\paragraph{Matching the order does not prove the code is right.} Both
papers print nested fractions that can be read in more than one way, and
several wrong readings still converge at order 8. Measuring the order
would not have caught them. Each paper's eighth-order \emph{error
constant} did. We drove each scheme one step from a point $10^{-24}$ away
from the root and compared the measured constant with the published
formula; our implementations agree to better than $10^{-20}$ in relative
terms. One wrong grouping in NWM11's $w'$ missed the constant by about 60
orders of magnitude while still converging at order 8. A separate check
caught test functions that had been mis-copied from the PDFs, where
superscripts had detached during text extraction (six of the eight in the
NWM9 paper): the root the paper publishes no longer zeroed our copy of the
function.
As a further check, setting every self-accelerating parameter to zero
gives order $8$ to within $10^{-3}$ on all eight functions for both methods, which
confirms that the extra order comes from the memory.

\paragraph{The right root, not just a small residual.} A solver can
converge to a wrong branch and still report a tiny residual. We ran each
solver from $E_0 = M$ on 4{,}975 points against the 50-digit reference
(Table~\ref{tab:correct}). We call a converged solve a wrong root if it
lands more than $10^{-6}$ from the reference. No solver returned one. The
errors of order $10^{-10}$ come from points where $f'$ is small: the
stopping rule tests the residual, and the error in $E$ is roughly
$f/f'$. Where a solver does not converge it uses up its iteration limit (100 in
this check) and says so. That outcome is visible and can be retried, unlike a wrong root,
so we treat it as a robustness problem in Section~\ref{sec:failure}.

\paragraph{Iterations.} The with-memory methods need the fewest
iterations. The median solve takes one iteration for NWM9 and NWM11, two
for Danby and three for Newton. The higher order is real and visible.

\paragraph{Cost.} In measured units, the median with-memory solve costs
$6.34$ against Danby's $7.68$, about 17\% less. The mean reverses this:
$9.55$ for NWM11 and $10.04$ for NWM9 against $7.48$ for Danby, 28\% and
34\% more. Both results follow from how the calls add up. Every solve pays
one lone $\sin$ for the residual at the guess and one per iteration for the
stopping test. Danby's typical solve is two iterations, each with one
$(\sin,\cos)$ pair, so it costs $2(2.34) + 3 = 7.68$. The typical
with-memory solve is one iteration with one pair and two lone $\sin$ calls
inside the step, so it costs $2.34 + 2 + 2 = 6.34$. In such a solve there
is no previous iterate, so the parameters keep the fixed starting values
given in the papers and the step is a plain eighth-order one. The median
saving therefore comes from the eighth-order step, not from the memory,
which is also why NWM9 and NWM11 tie. The memory starts in the second
iteration, which 33\% of NWM11 solves and 39\% of NWM9 solves need. A full
with-memory iteration costs $10.62$ against $3.34$ for a classical one,
because it adds three interpolated derivatives at $1.76$ each
(Figure~\ref{fig:costbd}), and these second iterations push the mean above
Danby's. Under call counting the two kinds of iteration cost $4$ and $2$.

gives the same ranking: 4.5\,$\mu$s for Danby, 5.3 for NWM11 and 6.6 for
NWM9. For the median solve, then, the model says the with-memory methods
are cheaper and the clock says they are slower.
Section~\ref{sec:sincos} explains why.

\subsubsection{The failures come from the memory}

As $e \to 1$ and $M \to 0$, the root $E$ goes to zero, $\cos E \to 1$, and
$f'(E) = 1 - e\cos E$ goes to zero. At $e = 0.9999$, $f'(0) = 10^{-4}$.
A Newton-type step divides by this derivative, so a small error in the
iterate becomes a large jump. All Newton-type methods feel this, which is
why Newton and even Danby have a few failures here. The with-memory
methods have a second problem on top of it. Take NWM11's first step. With
$\alpha_k = -H_5''(s_k)/(2f'(s_k))$ its denominator is
\begin{equation}
f'(s_k) + \alpha_k f(s_k) = f'(s_k)\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{2}L_k\right),
\qquad L_k = \frac{f(s_k)\,H_5''(s_k)}{f'(s_k)^2}.
\label{eq:halley}
\end{equation}
If $H_5''$ were the exact $f''$, this step would be Halley's method. $L_k$
is the Newton step $f/f'$ multiplied by a second factor $H_5''/f'$, so it
grows faster than the Newton step as $f'$ shrinks. As $L_k$ approaches 2
the denominator vanishes, and beyond 2 it changes sign and the step points
away from the root. $H_5''$ makes this worse. It is fitted through the
previous iterates, and after a large step those can lie far from the
current point, so $H_5''$ need not resemble $f''$ there.
NWM9's $\alpha_n$ contains the same term $-H_5''/(2f')$. For $\alpha_k$,
the guard of Equation~\eqref{eq:guard} is exactly the condition
$|L_k| \le 1$.

We tested this explanation directly by running both methods with the
memory switched off: every parameter fixed at zero, which gives the
eighth-order parent methods. Table~\ref{tab:fix} shows the result.
With the memory off, all 74 of NWM11's failed solves converge, and 79 of
NWM9's 81. The memoryless methods fail instead on 9 and 18 solves, and
only two of those are points where the published method also fails. The
mean iteration count barely changes. The memory therefore causes nearly
all of the published methods' failures, though the base methods are not
free of trouble in the corner either.

\subsubsection{NWM9 overshoots from a poor guess}
\label{sec:orderdrop}

The order scan on Kepler's equation itself (Table~\ref{tab:ordere},
Figure~\ref{fig:ordere}) shows a weakness of NWM9 that has nothing to do
with its parameter. Each run starts from $E_0 = M = 0.3$ and takes ten
iterations. NWM11 keeps its order of about $10.7$ all the way to
$e = 0.999$. NWM9's values at $e = 0.9$ and $e \ge 0.99$ are not
convergence orders. In those runs NWM9 reached the root only on the last
iteration or not at all, so the estimator was fed the errors of a
wandering iterate. Newton's $2.13$ and $1.79$ have the same cause.

The wandering starts in NWM9's second sub-step,
$z = x - t\,[1 + u + (1 + \frac{1}{1+t})\,u^2]$ with $t = f(x)/f'(x)$ and
$u = f(y)/f(x)$, which contains no parameter. The iteration history we
saved with the grid benchmark at $e = 0.99$, $M = 0.1$ shows how. From
$E_0 = M$, $f'(E_0) = 0.015$, so the Newton sub-step lands at
$y \approx 6.7$ and $u \approx -63$. The $u^2$ term makes the bracket about
$3{,}200$, and the first iterate is $E_1 \approx 2.1\times10^{4}$. From the
canonical guess the same run reaches $|E| \approx 10^{27}$ and fails. The
overshoot happens in the first iteration, before any memory exists, so the
guard cannot prevent it. What the memory changes is the recovery. The next
parameter is fitted through iterates that lie far apart, so it no longer
describes $f$ near the root, and the run keeps wandering. With the memory
off, NWM9 converges on 79 of the 81 solves where the published version
fails (Section~\ref{sec:failure}). A better guess avoids the overshoot
altogether: from Napier's guess NWM9 converges on all 7{,}400 grid points
(Section~\ref{sec:guess}).

\subsubsection{The cost model's premise fails in Python}
\label{sec:sincos}

Section~\ref{sec:grid} found the with-memory methods cheaper in the cost
model but slower on the clock. The cost argument for these methods assumes
that one call returns both $\sin E$ and $\cos E$ for about the price of one. In our measurements a
pair costs $2.34$, against $2.04$ for a $\sin$ and a $\cos$ timed
separately, so in Python there is no discount. CPython has no fused
\texttt{sincos}: \texttt{math.sin} and \texttt{math.cos} are two
separate C calls. The one call that returns both,
\texttt{cmath.exp(1j*x)}, costs about six \texttt{sin} calls. Interpolated
derivatives are also expensive in Python, at $1.76$ \texttt{sin} units
each, because a few interpreted arithmetic operations cost more than one C
call. This is what turns a cheaper model cost into a slower clock time
(Figure~\ref{fig:wall}). In a compiled kernel such as RadVel's, both
effects would shrink, so the papers' reasoning may well hold there.

solves (88\% of points, cost at most 15) the correlation falls to
$r = 0.42$.}

\paragraph{NWM11.} The guard cuts NWM11's failures from 74 to 3. That is

many as switching the memory off leaves (18). The remaining failures are
mostly new ones: only 2 of the 17 also fail with the memory off, and the
published NWM9 converges on 11 of them. All 17 start from the simple or canonical guess at $e > 0.94$,

guard does not act on it. The guard keeps NWM9's order on its test
functions ($8.93$), and with Napier's guess both the published and the
guarded NWM9 converge on every point.

The guard is the improvement we propose to the base papers: a test that
costs two multiplications per parameter, needs no new evaluations, and
leaves the method unchanged wherever the papers' analysis applies. On
Kepler's equation it brings NWM11 close to Danby's robustness (3 failures
against 2) while keeping its lower iteration count.

\subsection{Does solver error matter downstream?}
\label{sec:downstream}

We traced solver error from $E$ through the true anomaly $\nu$ and the
radial velocity $v_r$ into the fitted orbit. The question is how large
the solver's effect is compared with the uncertainty that is already in
the data. For each solver we refit the same injected dataset at six
tolerances, from $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-14}$, and measure how far each fitted
parameter moves from that solver's own fit at $10^{-14}$.
Table~\ref{tab:budget} compares the largest such shift with the spread
caused by noise across 50 refits with fresh noise, and with RadVel's own
MCMC posterior width.

Even at a tolerance of $10^{-4}$, far looser than anyone would use, the
solver moves the fit about 1{,}500 times less than the noise does
(1{,}490 times for $e$, the closest case). The sizes involved explain
why. An error $\delta E$ changes the velocity by at most
$K\,(d\nu/dE)\,\delta E$, and for this orbit $d\nu/dE \le 1.44$, so an
error of $10^{-4}$ in $E$ moves $v_r$ by at most
$7\times10^{-4}$\,m\,s$^{-1}$. The measurement uncertainties are 0.8 to
3.0\,m\,s$^{-1}$.

Only Newton at $10^{-4}$ shows a clear solver effect, $6.4\times10^{-4}$
posterior widths in $e$. Newton's last step can stop with $|f|$ just
under the tolerance, which leaves an error of about $10^{-4}$ in $E$; a
fourth-order or higher step that passes the same test lands many orders
of magnitude below it. Every other combination stays at or below about
$10^{-5}$ posterior widths, and this floor does not shrink as the
tolerance tightens. It comes from the optimiser's own stopping precision,
not from the solver: at $10^{-14}$ the five solvers' fits still differ
from each other by up to $1.2\times10^{-7}$ in $e$, about
$7\times10^{-6}$ posterior widths. Below $10^{-4}$ we can therefore only
bound the solver's effect, not measure it (Figure~\ref{fig:tol}). The
shift is zero at $10^{-14}$ by construction, and Markley, which has no
tolerance, gives the same fit at every setting.

The spread across the 50 noisy refits agrees with the MCMC posterior
width to within 3\% for $K$, 5\% for $e$, 9\% for $\omega$ and 21\% for
$P$. The two estimates of the noise are computed independently, so their
agreement checks both.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We reimplemented two recent with-memory methods, confirmed that they
reach their published orders, and tested them on Kepler's equation against
the methods astronomers already use. Their high order is real: they need
fewer iterations than any other iterative method, and with our measured
weights their median solve is 17\% cheaper than Danby's. That saving
comes from one-iteration solves, where the memory is not yet used. It
does not hold on average, because the long solves in the corner cost
more, and in Python it does not show up on the clock.

Their weakness is the corner where $f' \approx 0$. There the
self-accelerating parameter, derived for iterates already near the root,
becomes as large as the derivative it corrects and throws the step far
from the root.
Switching the memory off showed that this, not the base method, causes
most failures. A safeguard that keeps the parameter only while its
correction is less than half of $|f'|$ removes almost all of NWM11's
failures and leaves its order and iteration count unchanged. With this
change NWM11 fails on 3 of 29{,}600 solves against Danby's 2, and still
needs fewer iterations (1.38 against 1.94, each at its best guess). Separately, a better starting guess is the cheapest
improvement of all: Napier's guess helps every solver on most of the
domain, and for NWM9 it is worth more than changing the method.

Downstream, the solver does not change the fitted orbit in any way that
matters: even Newton stopped at $10^{-4}$ moves it about 1{,}500 times
less than the measurement noise does. A new solver therefore has to win
on speed. In Python it does not, so Danby or Markley remain the practical
choice. Our call counts do not promise a win in compiled code either:
with one call per $(\sin,\cos)$ pair and free arithmetic, the median solve
costs five calls for Danby, NWM9 and NWM11 alike, and the mean is higher
for guarded NWM11 (6.3 against 4.9 for Danby).

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Python only.} All timings are in CPython, which has no fused
      \texttt{sincos} and makes arithmetic expensive. This works against
      the with-memory methods specifically.
\item \textbf{One machine.} The cost weights were measured on one machine,
      under CPython 3.14, while the benchmarks ran under CPython 3.12 on
      the same machine; the weights may differ elsewhere. Each RadVel fit was timed once, and
      the six fits for one solver vary by up to 1.0\,s (Danby's took
      0.91 to 1.88\,s), so per-fit times separate only NWM9 from Newton
      and Markley.
\item \textbf{The guard constant.} We set $c = 1/2$ from the guard's
      own algebra (Section~\ref{sec:guard}), did not tune it, and report
      no other value. The guard has been tested only on our grid.
\item \textbf{NWM9's remaining failures.} The guard leaves 17 NWM9
      failures, all from the simple or canonical guess at $e > 0.94$. It
      does not act on NWM9's second sub-step, which has no parameter.
\item \textbf{One injected orbit.} The downstream study uses one orbit
      with $e = 0.35$, and its posterior widths come from one short
      RadVel MCMC run, capped at 2000 steps. The margin over noise is large, but more eccentric
      orbits were not tested.
\item \textbf{The incumbent.} RadVel's compiled Danby, with its own guess
      and a $10^{-12}$ tolerance, was not run under our cost counting. Our
      ``Danby'' is our Python version at $10^{-14}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future work}

The most direct next step is a compiled version. With a fused
\texttt{sincos}, our call counts put guarded NWM11 level with Danby on a
typical solve and behind it on average, so a C or Cython port would show
whether the with-memory methods can win on time at all.
Second, NWM10, the one-parameter method from the same paper as NWM11,
would be the right control for measuring what the second parameter adds;
NWM9 comes from a different paper with a different base method. Third,
the guard constant should be checked on new test points and on other
equations with small derivatives, to see whether the safeguard helps
with-memory methods in general. Finally, injecting more eccentric orbits
would test the downstream result where the solver has the most chance to
matter.
